Short-Term Reversal Persists 
Globally—If Properly Measured 
 
Jan Stosik † and Adam Zaremba ‡* 
 
Abstract 
Short-term reversal appears to have weakened in international equity markets because 
standard measures mix ﬁrm-speciﬁc and industry-wide returns. When evaluated relative 
to industry peers, the reversal persists. Using data from 64 countries, the conventional 
strategy delivers negligible returns, whereas an industry-adjusted strategy earns 0.53% 
per month with a Sharpe ratio of 0.74. The eﬀect reﬂects the reversal of ﬁrm-speciﬁc 
shocks masked by persistent industry components. A regret-based signal captures the 
same industry-relative variation and adds no independent return. Reversal did not 
disappear; it was mismeasured. 
Keywords: short-term reversal, industry-adjusted reversal, intra-industry reversal, regret 
anomaly, international equity markets, return predictability, cross-section of stock 
returns 
JEL Codes: G12, C53, C55

1. Introduction 
Short-term reversal once ranked among the most reliable cross-sectional patterns in 
equity returns (Jegadeesh, 1990; Lehmann, 1990). Recent evidence paints a diﬀerent 
picture. In many markets—especially large and liquid ones—the eﬀect appears to have 
weakened or even disappeared (McLean & Pontiﬀ, 2016; Chordia et al., 2014; Chen & 
Velikov, 2023; Blitz et al., 2024). This shift raises a central question: has short-term 
reversal vanished, or do standard measures fail to detect it? 
We show that the apparent decline reﬂects measurement, not economic decay. When 
recent returns are evaluated relative to industry peers, short-term reversal remains 
economically large and statistically signiﬁcant across global equity markets. In contrast, 
the conventional signal based on raw returns delivers negligible returns. Raw returns 
combine ﬁrm-speciﬁc movements and industry-wide shocks. Firm-speciﬁc shocks tend to 
reverse, while industry components often persist (Da et al., 2014; Hameed & Mian, 2015; 
Blitz et al., 2024). When these forces oﬀset each other, the standard signal attenuates 
predictability. Removing the industry component restores the eﬀect. 
We test this idea using monthly stock-level data from 64 countries over 1990–2023. We 
compare three signals that diﬀer only in how they benchmark recent performance: (i) 
standard reversal based on raw returns, (ii) industry -adjusted reversal based on 
deviations from industry means, and (iii) a maximum-based signal motivated by the 
regret anomaly of Arisoy et al. (2024), deﬁned relative to the best-performing peer within 
the same industry. The last two signals, therefore, represent two ways to improve the 
standard reversal measure using industry-relative performance. We form long–short 
portfolios based on each signal and evaluate their performance using standard return 
and alpha measures. We then examine whether the regret-based signal captures distinct 
information or a narrower behavioral version of the broader industry-adjusted eﬀect. 
The evidence is clear. In the pooled global sample, the standard reversal strategy earns 
0.05% per month with an insigniﬁcant alpha, while the industry-adjusted strategy earns 
0.53% per month, with a Sharpe ratio of 0.74 and a six-factor alpha of 0.60% (t = 4.14). 
The contrast is strongest in large markets. In the United Kingdom and China, standard 
reversal yields negligible returns, whereas the industry-adjusted strategy earns 0.87% 
and 0.99% per month, respectively. Figure 1 illustrates the diﬀerence.

This framework also clariﬁes the relationship between industry-adjusted reversal and the 
regret-based signal of Arisoy et al. (2024). Both signals rank stocks by relative 
underperformance within industries, but they use diﬀerent benchmarks: the industry 
mean in one case and the industry winner in the other. In the global sample, the regret

strategy earns 0.40% per month, indicating that a behavioral, peer-based reﬁnement also 
improves upon the standard reversal signal. Yet once we control for industry-adjusted 
reversal, the regret alpha falls to 0.25% (t = 1.34). The evidence, therefore, points to a 
common underlying eﬀect: the regret signal does not add independent information; it 
reﬂects the same cross-sectional variation captured by industry-adjusted reversal. 
Related literature. Our paper relates to the short-term reversal literature initiated by 
Jegadeesh (1990) and Lehmann (1990). Reversal has been studied extensively in U.S. 
markets (Nagel, 2012; Hameed & Mian, 2015; Blitz et al., 2024), internationally 
(Jegadeesh et al., 2025), and in speciﬁc settings such as China (Chen et al., 2022; 
Neszveda et al., 2022) and international markets (Butt et al., 2021, 2024; Butt & 
Sadaqat, 2024; Kang et al., 2025). Prior work links reversal to liquidity provision 
(Campbell et al., 1993; Avramov et al., 2006; Cheng et al., 2017; Da et al., 2014) and 
behavioral mechanisms, including overreaction (De Bondt & Thaler, 1985; Heyman et 
al., 2019), sentiment (Daniel et al., 1998; Dyl et al., 2019), and under-inference (Farkas 
& Neszveda, 2025). We build most directly on Da et al. (2014) and Blitz et al. (2024), 
extending their industry-based approach to 64 international markets. 
Furthermore, our evidence speaks to anomaly attenuation (McLean & Pontiﬀ, 2016; 
Chen & Zimmermann, 2022; Chordia et al., 2014; Chen & Velikov, 2023; Gordon et al., 
2025; Brogaard & Zarei, 2023). We relate most closely to international studies (Jacobs 
& Müller, 2020; Hollstein, 2022) and are the ﬁrst to focus on short-term reversal in this 
context. Our results align with Jacobs and Müller (2020), showing that anomalies need 
not disappear when carefully measured. 
Finally, we contribute to the literature on the regret anomaly (Arisoy et al., 2024), 
studied in the U.S. and Japan (Kim & Ohk, 2025), China (Wang et al., 2026), and 
globally (Atilgan et al., 2025). Our evidence places that anomaly in a broader framework: 
regret-based reversal also improves on the raw reversal signal because it evaluates stocks 
relative to industry peers, but across 64 markets, its return largely reﬂects the same 
industry-relative variation captured by the more general industry-adjusted measure. 
The remainder of the paper proceeds as follows. Section 2 describes the data  and 
methods. Section 3 presents the ﬁndings. Finally, Section 4 concludes. 
2. Data and Methods 
We use monthly stock-level data for common equities across 64 developed and emerging 
markets from January 1990 to December 2023. For each stock -month, the dataset 
includes excess returns, market capitalization, industry identiﬁers used to construct 
relative variables, ﬁrm characteristics for factor construction, lagged returns, and 
industry-adjusted performance measures. U.S. data are from CRSP, while international

data construction and express all variables in U.S. dollars using Compustat exchange 
rates, consistent with standard practice in international asset pricing (e.g., Fama and 
French, 2012, 2017). 
We restrict the sample to common stocks listed on the main exchanges. At the stock 
level, we exclude observations with missing returns, prices ≤ 1, missing industry codes, 
and nano-cap ﬁrms. At the country-month level, we require at least 10 stocks per market.

3. Results 
This section presents the main results. We ﬁrst compare standard and industry-adjusted 
reversal, then examine robustness and scrutinize the link between intra-industry reversal 
and the regret-anomaly-based reﬁnement.

3.1. Baseline Findings 
Table 1 reports the performance of standard and industry-adjusted short-term reversal 
across 64 equity markets and in the pooled global sample. The contrast is remarkable. 
The standard reversal strategy yields weak, often insigniﬁcant results, whereas the 
industry-adjusted alternative remains economically large and statistically signiﬁcant, 
consistent with U.S. evidence (Blitz et al., 2024) and extending it internationally. 
[Insert Table 1 here] 
In the pooled global sample, the standard strategy generates an average monthly return 
of 0.05% with a six-factor alpha of 0.18%, neither statistically signiﬁcant. At the country 
level, signiﬁcant returns appear in only 13 of 64 markets, mostly smaller or less liquid 
economies such as Chile, Peru, and the Philippines. In large markets, performance 
remains weak: 0.01% per month in the United States, 0.15% in the United Kingdom, 
and 0.16% in Germany, none statistically signiﬁcant. 
By contrast, the industry-adjusted strategy earns 0.53% per month, with a Sharpe ratio 
of 0.74 and a six-factor alpha of 0.60% (t = 5.02 and 4.14). This represents a substantial 
improvement over the standard signal. The strategy produces statistically signiﬁcant 
returns and alphas in 22 of 64 markets, including large and liquid economies such as 
France (0.68%), Japan (0.82%), the United Kingdom (0.87%), and the United States 
(0.34%). 
3.2. Robustness checks 
Table 2 examines robustness across time and ﬁrm-size groups. The industry-adjusted 
strategy delivers positive and statistically signiﬁcant returns across subperiods, micro-, 
small-, and large-cap stocks, and in the global equal-weighted sample, while the standard 
strategy remains weak. 
[Insert Table 2 here] 
Performance is stronger in the ﬁrst half of the sample but remains economically and 
statistically signiﬁcant in the second, indicating attenuation rather than disappearance. 
Returns are higher among micro and small caps, yet remain signiﬁcant for large ﬁrms, 
suggesting the eﬀect does not rely on a single segment. Equal-weighted results point in 
the same direction. Table 2 reports global equal-weighted portfolios, while detailed 
country-level results appear in Table A2 in the Online Appendix. Overall, the advantage 
of the industry-adjusted strategy holds across periods, ﬁrm sizes, and weighting schemes, 
indicating that short-term reversal persists once returns are measured relative to industry 
peers.

3.3. Regret-Based Reversal as an Industry-Relative Reﬁnement 
Arisoy et al. (2024) construct a regret-based signal as the deviation between a stock’s 
return and the maximum return within its industry. That signal ﬁts naturally into our 
framework. Both regret-based reversal and industry-adjusted reversal improve on the 
standard speciﬁcation by evaluating recent performance relative to industry peers rather 
than in isolation. They diﬀer only in the benchmark: the industry-adjusted signal uses 
the peer average, whereas the regret signal uses the industry winner. Implicitly, regret 
may capture a sharper, behaviorally motivated version of the same underlying industry-
relative reversal. 
Table 3 supports this interpretation. Panel A shows that the regret strategy is 
economically meaningful on its own. In the pooled global sample, it earns 0.40% per 
month with an alpha of 0.39%. Several markets exhibit signiﬁcant returns, although the 
eﬀect is less widespread than for the industry-adjusted strategy. Benchmarking stocks 
against industry peers, whether using the mean or the best performer, improves the raw 
reversal signal. 
[Insert Table 3 here] 
Panel B sharpens the distinction between the two signals. If regret captured an 
independent return premium, the intercept would remain large after controlling for the 
industry-adjusted strategy. It does not. In the pooled sample, the spanning alpha falls 
to 0.25% (t = 1.34), while the loading on industry-adjusted reversal remains positive 
and signiﬁcant, and the regression delivers an R² of 0.05, with higher explanatory power 
in several individual markets. The regret signal does not introduce a separate source of 
predictability beyond the industry-adjusted measure. Instead, regret returns co-move 
with industry-adjusted reversal, consistent with both signals ranking stocks on the same 
industry-relative dimension, with the regret measure placing more weight on extreme 
peer comparisons. 
The cross-country evidence corroborates these conclusions. Only 5 markets exhibit a 
signiﬁcant spanning alpha, whereas most markets show insigniﬁcant intercepts once 
industry-adjusted reversal is included. By contrast, exposure to the industry-adjusted 
strategy remains strong across a broad set of countries. Additional tests in the Online 
Appendix conﬁrm the same pattern. Equal-weighted results are similar (Table A3), 
bidirectional spanning tests show that industry-adjusted reversal subsumes the regret 
strategy whereas the reverse does not (Table A4), and the relation persists across 
subperiods and ﬁrm-size groups (Table A5). 
Our results place the regret premium inside the broader industry-adjusted reversal eﬀect. 
Regret-based reversal improves on the raw signal by evaluating stocks against industry

peers, but it does not stand apart from the more general industry-adjusted measure. The 
industry-adjusted speciﬁcation remains our primary measure, while the regret signal 
oﬀers a behavioral interpretation built around extreme peer comparisons. 
4. Concluding Remarks 
Industry-adjusted short-term reversal remains strong across international markets, 
whereas the standard measure delivers weak results. The eﬀect survives across subperiods 
and speciﬁcations. A regret-based signal also improves on the standard reversal measure 
by evaluating stocks relative to industry peers, but its return largely reﬂects the broader 
industry-adjusted eﬀect. The apparent decline of short-term reversal, therefore, reﬂects 
measurement, not economic decay.
